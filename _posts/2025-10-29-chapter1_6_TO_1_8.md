<h1 style="color:#9a3ba6;"><b>Function Prologue and Epilogue</b></h1>

<hr>

<h2 style="color:#3ba2a6;"><b>The Function Prologue</b></h2>

<p>
It consists of a series of instructions at the beginning of the function.
</p>

<p>
And most of the time, it looks like this part of the code:
</p>

<p>
Let’s talk about this code step by step:
</p>

<p>
The first thing it does is that it pushes the value of <b style = "color : chartreuse">ebp</b> onto the stack.
</p>

<p>
Then, it makes the value of <b style = "color : chartreuse">esp</b> equal to <b style = "color : chartreuse">ebp</b>.
</p>

<p>
After that, it allocates space for the variable on the stack.
</p>

<p>
Keep in mind that the value of <b style = "color : chartreuse">ebp</b> remains the same during the execution of the function,
and it’s used to access local variables and arguments.
</p>

<p>
For the same purpose, it’s also possible to use <b style = "color : chartreuse">ESP</b>,
but since it changes over time, it’s inconvenient to use.
</p>

<hr>

<h2 style="color:#3ba2a6;"><b>The Function Epilogue</b></h2>

<p>
Here, simply, it does exactly the opposite.  
In short, let me tell you:
</p>

<p>
It releases the space that was allocated in the stack,  
restores the <b style = "color : chartreuse">EBP</b> to its original state,  
and returns the control flow to the caller of the function:
</p>

</code>

<p>
Usually, the prologues and epilogues of functions are recognized by disassemblers.
</p>

<p>
Also, prologues and epilogues can negatively affect the performance of recursive functions (those that call themselves).
</p>

<p>
Then the author started connecting this part with the previous examples we discussed earlier about <b>Empty Function</b> and <b>Returning Values</b>.
</p>
